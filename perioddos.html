Colonial America

1607 – 1754

Thirteen colonies take shape along the Atlantic coast, each one different from the last. Tobacco, Puritans, slaves, fur traders, and religious refugees all end up on the same continent — and somehow Britain thinks it can hold all of it together from 3,000 miles away.

Why the English Were Different

Spain came to extract. France came to trade. England came to stay. That's the core difference that shapes everything in Period 2. English colonists brought families, built towns, planted fields, and had no intention of going home. This produced a fundamentally different kind of colonization — more permanent, more demographically dense, and eventually much harder for Britain to control.

The Joint-Stock Company made it financially possible. The Virginia Company of London pooled investor money to fund Jamestown in 1607. When tobacco became profitable in the 1610s under John Rolfe, it saved the colony and locked the Chesapeake into a plantation economy that would define it for two centuries.

The Four Colonial Regions

New England (Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire) was built by Puritans who wanted to create a model Christian community. John Winthrop's "City Upon a Hill" speech from 1630 is the founding document of that idea. The climate was too cold for large-scale agriculture, so the economy ran on fishing, lumber, and small-scale farming. Towns were tight-knit, literacy was high so people could read the Bible, and life expectancy was the best in the colonies.

The Middle Colonies (New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware) were the most diverse. Pennsylvania, founded by Quaker William Penn in 1681, attracted Germans, Scots-Irish, Dutch, and English settlers because of its religious tolerance. The "breadbasket" colonies grew grain for export. If you remember one thing: religious tolerance plus diversity equals Middle Colonies.

The Chesapeake (Virginia, Maryland) ran on tobacco, and tobacco ran on labor. Early on that labor came from indentured servants — poor English who traded 4–7 years of work for passage to America. The Headright System gave 50 acres to anyone who paid a servant's passage, which meant wealthy planters accumulated enormous tracts. Life expectancy was terrible. Malaria and disease killed so many early settlers that the Chesapeake had a persistently skewed male-to-female ratio for decades.

The Deep South (Carolina, Georgia) developed last and went hardest on plantation agriculture. Rice and indigo required intensive labor in hot, wet conditions. Enslaved Africans from rice-growing regions of West Africa — the "Rice Coast" — were specifically sought for their agricultural expertise. South Carolina's enslaved population outnumbered white colonists by the early 1700s.

Bacon's Rebellion and the Shift to Slavery

1676. Nathaniel Bacon leads a coalition of poor white farmers and freed indentured servants — newly landless, angry, and armed — in an uprising against Virginia's colonial government. They burn Jamestown. The rebellion fails when Bacon dies of dysentery, but it scares the planter class badly enough to rethink the whole labor system. Indentured servants who finished their contracts became a dangerous underclass. Enslaved Africans couldn't finish a contract and demand land. The shift from indentured servitude to chattel slavery accelerates sharply after 1676. This causation chain is one APUSH tests constantly.

The Great Awakening and the Enlightenment

The First Great Awakening (1730s–40s) was a wave of emotional, populist religious revivalism led by Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield. It challenged established church hierarchies, created new denominations like Baptists and Methodists, and taught ordinary people that they could question authority. That's a short step from questioning a minister to questioning a king.

The Enlightenment moved through print culture. John Locke's ideas about natural rights and the social contract circulated among educated colonists. Benjamin Franklin embodied the colonial Enlightenment — practical, rational, and deeply skeptical of inherited authority. Both movements together created the ideological conditions for revolution, even if that's technically Period 3.

Salutary Neglect and Self-Government

Britain's policy of salutary neglect — not enforcing trade laws and leaving colonies alone — ran from roughly the 1690s to the 1760s. In that time, colonies built their own representative assemblies, developed their own legal traditions, and got very comfortable running their own affairs. When Britain tried to tighten control after the French and Indian War, colonists had decades of self-governance habits that made the new rules feel like tyranny. Salutary neglect is the direct setup for the Revolution.

Native Americans in Period 2

King Philip's War (1675–76) was the bloodiest conflict per capita in American history. Wampanoag leader Metacom organized a coalition against English expansion in New England. The war effectively ended organized Native resistance in the region. Meanwhile the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 in New Mexico — led by Popé — successfully expelled the Spanish for 12 years. The only successful large-scale indigenous rebellion against European colonizers in North American history. Spain came back in 1692 but was more cautious afterward.

Essential Terms

Joint-Stock Company
Investors pool money to fund colonial ventures. The Virginia Company funded Jamestown. Risk is shared — so is profit, if it ever comes.
Indentured Servitude
Poor Europeans trade 4–7 years of labor for passage to America. Primary Chesapeake labor source before slavery takes over after 1676.
Headright System
50 acres to anyone who paid for an immigrant's passage to Virginia. Wealthy planters accumulated massive landholdings. Deepened inequality from day one.
Bacon's Rebellion (1676)
Poor whites and freed servants rebel in Virginia, burn Jamestown. Fail. But it terrifies planters into switching to enslaved African labor — who can't demand land at the end of a contract.
Salutary Neglect
Britain doesn't enforce trade laws or interfere in colonial governance for ~70 years. Colonies get so used to self-rule that British control attempts later feel like tyranny.
Puritans
English Calvinists who settled New England to build a "godly" community. Valued literacy, hard work, communal conformity. Intolerant of dissent — see Roger Williams, Anne Hutchinson.
First Great Awakening
Religious revival of the 1730s–40s. Edwards, Whitefield. Broke church hierarchies, taught ordinary people to question authority. Gateway to revolutionary thinking.
The Enlightenment
European philosophical movement: reason, natural rights, social contract. Locke's ideas circulate through colonial print culture. Gives educated colonists the vocabulary to argue against British rule.
Middle Passage
Transatlantic leg of the slave trade. Africans packed into ships for weeks or months. Mortality rates 10–20%. Survivors arrived traumatized into a life of chattel slavery.
Anglicization
Colonies become more culturally "British" through trade, print, and law. Paradoxically, this made colonists more aware of English rights — and more angry when Britain denied them.
Mercantilism
Colonies exist to enrich the mother country. Navigation Acts required colonists to trade through Britain. Salutary neglect meant these weren't enforced — until they suddenly were.
Roger Williams / Anne Hutchinson
Both banished from Massachusetts Bay for challenging Puritan authority. Williams founded Rhode Island with actual religious tolerance. Both show that Puritan conformity had real teeth.

Key Events Timeline

1607
Jamestown Founded
First permanent English settlement. Virginia Company funded it. Half the settlers die the first winter. Tobacco (1612) saves it.
1619
Two Things at Once
Virginia House of Burgesses — first representative assembly in English America. And the first Africans arrive in Virginia, initially as indentured servants, not yet as chattel slaves.
1620
Mayflower Compact
Pilgrims agree to govern themselves before leaving the ship. Early step toward self-governance and consent of the governed. Not Puritans — Separatists who wanted a clean break from the Church of England.
1630
Winthrop's "City Upon a Hill"
John Winthrop delivers his vision for Massachusetts Bay as a model Christian community. Sets the religious tone for New England. Referenced by politicians ever since.
1636
Roger Williams Founds Rhode Island
Banished from Massachusetts for arguing church and state should be separate. Rhode Island becomes the first colony with genuine religious tolerance.
1675–76
King Philip's (Metacom's) War
Bloodiest war per capita in American history. Wampanoag coalition vs. English colonists. Metacom killed and beheaded. Native resistance in New England effectively over.
1676
Bacon's Rebellion
Poor whites and landless freed servants rebel in Virginia. Burn Jamestown. Fail. Planters pivot to enslaved African labor permanently.
1680
Pueblo Revolt
Popé leads Pueblo peoples to expel the Spanish for 12 years — the only successful large-scale indigenous rebellion in North American history. Spain returns in 1692 but more carefully.
1730s–40s
First Great Awakening
Jonathan Edwards, George Whitefield. Emotional revival preaching. Breaks up established churches, teaches colonists to question hierarchies. Ideological setup for revolution.
1754
Albany Plan + French & Indian War Begins
Franklin's failed plan to unite the colonies. The war starts and ends Period 2 — it changes everything about Britain's relationship with the colonies and launches Period 3.
DBQ Prompt
Evaluate the extent to which Bacon's Rebellion (1676) transformed the labor system of the Chesapeake colonies in the period 1650–1750.

Document 1

Source: William Berkeley, Governor of VirginiaLetter to the King, 1676
"How miserable that man is that Governes a People wher six parts of seaven at least are Poore Indebted Discontented and Armed... I am not able to tell your Majestie the condition of this unhappy Countrey."

Document 2

Source: Virginia Slave CodesVirginia General Assembly, 1705
"All servants imported and brought into this country by sea or land, who were not Christians in their native country... shall be accounted and be slaves, and as such be here bought and sold notwithstanding a conversion to Christianity afterwards."

Document 3

Source: Nathaniel BaconDeclaration of the People, 1676
"We appeal to the country itself what and of what nature their oppressions have been and by what cabal and mystery the designs of many of those whom we call great men have been transacted and carried on."

Document 4

Source: Edmund Morgan, HistorianAmerican Slavery, American Freedom, 1975
"Virginians may have had an easier time with the substitution of black for white labor, because the substitution solved so many problems at once. It enabled the planters to keep their lands, replace a dangerous free population with a controllable enslaved one, and maintain the same profitable tobacco economy."

Document 5

Source: Royal African Company RecordsEnglish Trade Statistics, 1672–1713
"Slaves transported to Virginia and Maryland: 1672–1680: approximately 2,000. 1690–1700: approximately 10,000. 1700–1710: approximately 18,000. The numbers reflect the accelerating demand for bound African labor in the tobacco colonies following the decline of indentured servant immigration."
Scoring Breakdown (7 points possible)
  • Thesis (1 pt) — Go beyond "Bacon's Rebellion changed things." Argue the degree and mechanism: "Bacon's Rebellion substantially accelerated the Chesapeake's shift to chattel slavery by demonstrating the existential danger of a landless white underclass, but the transition was already underway before 1676."
  • Contextualization (1 pt) — Describe the tobacco economy, the Headright System creating landless inequality, and the supply of indentured servants from England drying up in the 1670s. Connect it to the argument.
  • Evidence: Documents (2 pts) — Use at least 3 docs to address the topic (1 pt), or 6+ docs to directly support your argument (2 pts). The trade statistics in Doc 5 are your best quantitative evidence.
  • Outside Evidence (1 pt) — The 1662 Virginia law making slavery hereditary (partus sequitur ventrem), the Royal African Company's monopoly ending in 1698 opening the slave trade to all English merchants, or the Stono Rebellion (1739) showing the long-term social consequences.
  • Sourcing (1 pt) — At least 3 documents need POV/purpose/audience/situation analysis. Morgan (Doc 4) is a historian writing retrospectively with a specific argument — consider what he emphasizes and why. Berkeley (Doc 1) writes to his king, which shapes what he reveals.
  • Complexity (1 pt) — The 1662 hereditary slavery law predates Bacon's Rebellion. The shift to slavery was happening before 1676 — Bacon's Rebellion accelerated it but didn't start it. That nuance earns the complexity point.

Planning Help

Thesis FrameBacon's Rebellion accelerated rather than initiated the shift from indentured to enslaved labor — the planter class was already moving that direction, but 1676 made the choice feel urgent and permanent.
Complexity MoveThe 1662 Virginia law making slavery hereditary predates the rebellion by 14 years. Bacon's Rebellion is a catalyst, not an origin point. Say that explicitly.
Outside Evidence1698: Royal African Company loses monopoly. Slave trade opens to all English merchants. Prices drop, volume skyrockets. This is what makes mass-scale enslaved labor economically feasible.
LEQ Prompt
Evaluate the extent to which the development of distinct regional identities in British North America between 1607 and 1754 was shaped by economic factors.
Scoring Breakdown (6 points possible)
  • Thesis (1 pt) — Economic factors were central but not the whole story. "While economic factors — particularly cash crop agriculture and trade — were the primary drivers of regional identity in colonial America, religious motivation and geographic environment also shaped each region's distinct character."
  • Contextualization (1 pt) — England's mercantilist system, the joint-stock company model, and the 17th-century push for colonial expansion all provide context. Explain how this framework created the conditions for economic variation across regions.
  • Evidence (2 pts) — Specific: tobacco plus Headright System in Chesapeake, grain economy of Middle Colonies, fishing/lumber in New England, rice/indigo in Carolina. Name specific colonies, specific crops, specific labor systems.
  • Historical Reasoning (1 pt) — Comparison works naturally here. Compare how two or three regions developed differently based on economic conditions. Or use causation: how did tobacco's labor demands cause the Chesapeake's specific social structure?
  • Complexity (1 pt) — Argue that economic and non-economic factors reinforced each other. Puritanism shaped New England's commercial culture. The Deep South's racial slave majority was both an economic choice and a social system that took on its own logic.

Evidence to Use

4–5 paragraphs. Don't do a formal conclusion — end on your strongest evidence or your complexity point.

SAQ Prompt — No Thesis Required
Use the following excerpt and your knowledge of United States history to answer parts A, B, and C.
Source: John Winthrop"A Model of Christian Charity," 1630
"We must consider that we shall be as a city upon a hill. The eyes of all people are upon us. So that if we shall deal falsely with our God in this work we have undertaken, and so cause Him to withdraw His present help from us, we shall be made a story and a by-word through the world."
Part A
Briefly explain ONE specific historical development that helps explain the context in which Winthrop delivered this speech in 1630.
Part B
Briefly explain ONE way in which Puritan New England society reflected the ideals expressed in Winthrop's speech during the period 1630–1700.
Part C
Briefly explain ONE way in which the development of New England society challenged or complicated Winthrop's vision of a unified, godly community.
SAQ Tips (3 points — 1 per part)
  • No thesis. No intro. Start with the actual answer and back it up with specific evidence.
  • Part A ideas: Archbishop Laud persecuting Puritans in England, Charles I dissolving Parliament, the Great Migration (1630–42) bringing ~20,000 Puritans to New England. Puritans felt England was beyond saving.
  • Part B ideas: Harvard College (1636) founded to train ministers — commitment to scripture-literate community. Town meeting governance shows communal self-organization. The Half-Way Covenant (1662) shows efforts to maintain religious community as the second generation drifted.
  • Part C ideas: Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson both banished — conformity enforced through expulsion, not genuine unity. Salem Witch Trials (1692) showed communal anxiety tearing the community apart. The Half-Way Covenant itself shows religious cohesion weakening by mid-century.
  • Each part needs a specific example. A name, a date, a law, an event. Vague answers don't score.
