Before Contact

Before 1492, the Americas were not empty. Hundreds of distinct Native American societies occupied the continent, each one shaped by its environment. Maize — corn — is arguably the most important plant in pre-contact North America. Its cultivation spread northward from Mexico over centuries and turned nomadic groups into settled ones, which made everything else possible: larger populations, trade networks, social hierarchies, and eventually the complex confederacies that Europeans would stumble into.

The Iroquois Confederacy in the Northeast, the Pueblo peoples of the Southwest, the Mississippian mound builders of the interior — these were not primitive societies. They had political structures, trade routes spanning hundreds of miles, and religious systems that rivaled anything in Europe in terms of complexity. The exam loves to test whether you understand this. "Pre-contact" does not mean "prehistoric."

Columbus and the Columbian Exchange

1492 is not just a date on a test — it's the moment two hemispheres that had been biologically isolated for tens of thousands of years suddenly got plugged into the same system. The Columbian Exchange moved plants, animals, and diseases across the Atlantic in both directions. Potatoes and corn went to Europe and eventually fed population explosions there. Horses and cattle went to the Americas and transformed Native life on the Great Plains. And smallpox — along with measles, typhus, and influenza — went to the Americas and killed somewhere between 50 and 90 percent of indigenous populations in certain regions. That last part is the one that made European colonization possible.

Spanish Empire

Spain moved fast. By the early 1500s they had Caribbean islands, then Mexico (the Aztec Empire fell to Hernán Cortés in 1521), then Peru (Inca Empire, Francisco Pizarro, 1532). The pattern was consistent: a small group of Spanish conquistadors + Indigenous allies who hated the dominant power + European disease doing most of the actual work = conquest.

The Encomienda System was how Spain extracted wealth. The crown granted a Spanish settler the right to indigenous labor from a specific area. In theory, the settler was supposed to Christianize and "protect" the natives. In practice it was forced labor, and it was brutal. Bartolomé de las Casas documented the abuses and spent decades arguing in Spain that indigenous people had souls and rights. The Valladolid Debate of 1550 was the formal showdown between his position and that of Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, who argued natives were "natural slaves." Spain eventually issued the New Laws of 1542 attempting to reform the system, but enforcement was weak and the abuses continued.

As the indigenous population collapsed from disease and overwork, Spain turned to West Africa for labor. This is the beginning of the transatlantic slave trade within the colonial context — not peripheral to Period 1, but central to understanding why it unfolds the way it does.

The Casta System

Spanish colonial society developed a rigid racial hierarchy based on ancestry. Peninsulares (born in Spain) at the top. Then Criollos/Creoles (Spanish descent, born in the Americas). Then Mestizos (Spanish + indigenous). Then Mulattoes (Spanish + African). Then indigenous people and enslaved Africans at the bottom. This system mattered because it determined legal rights, land ownership, and social status. It also planted the seeds of resentment that would fuel Latin American independence movements centuries later.

England and France: Slow Starters

England and France were busy with their own religious wars and internal politics while Spain was building an empire. England's first attempt at colonization — Roanoke in 1587, Sir Walter Raleigh's project — disappeared completely. No one knows what happened. Jamestown in 1607, which is technically Period 2, marks the actual beginning of permanent English presence in North America, and even that nearly failed multiple times.

France focused more on the fur trade than on settlement, which meant they tended to build relationships with Native groups rather than just trying to conquer them. This gave France a different kind of presence in North America — less territorial, more network-based, and ultimately more sustainable in the short term.

Essential Terms

Maize (Corn)
The crop that enabled nomadic tribes to settle permanently. Spread north from Mexico. Made larger populations and social hierarchies possible.
Columbian Exchange
Massive transfer of plants, animals, and diseases between Old and New Worlds after 1492. Disease alone may have killed 50–90% of some indigenous populations.
Encomienda System
Spanish labor system: crown grants settler the right to indigenous labor. Supposed to Christianize natives. Was basically slavery.
Casta System
Rigid racial hierarchy in Spanish colonies. Peninsulares → Creoles → Mestizos → Mulattoes → Indigenous/African. Determined legal rights and land.
Mercantilism
Economic theory: national power = wealth. Colonies exist to enrich the mother country. Drives all European colonial policy in this period.
Sovereignty
The right to govern yourself. Native Americans had it. Europeans didn't recognize it. The core conflict of the entire period.
Bartolomé de las Casas
Spanish friar who documented Spanish atrocities against indigenous people. Argued natives had souls and rights. Drove the Valladolid Debate.
Iroquois Confederacy
Political alliance of five (later six) nations in the Northeast: Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca. A sophisticated system of governance that some historians say influenced the U.S. Constitution.
Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)
Pope divides the New World between Spain and Portugal along a line. Spain gets most of the Americas. Portugal gets Brazil and Africa. Other European powers ignore it.
Syncretism
Blending of cultures — especially religious — when two groups meet. Native peoples often blended Catholic imagery with indigenous beliefs. Spain called it heresy. Historians call it adaptation.
Three Gs
Gold (wealth/resources), Glory (national prestige and personal fame), God (spreading Christianity). The three motivations driving Spanish and later European exploration.
Valladolid Debate (1550)
Las Casas vs. Sepúlveda. Are indigenous people fully human with rights, or natural slaves? Spain's answer shaped colonial law — even if enforcement was weak.

First sustained contact. Columbian Exchange begins. Caribbean Taíno people are the first to feel European colonization's full weight.

Pope divides the New World between Spain and Portugal. England, France, and the Dutch will later ignore this completely.

First Spanish legal code regulating treatment of indigenous people. Attempted reforms. Largely unenforced. More symbolic than effective.

Fall of the Aztec Empire
Cortés conquers Tenochtitlán with help from Indigenous allies and disease. Establishes the template for Spanish conquest elsewhere.
1542
New Laws of 1542
Las Casas's advocacy yields partial results. Laws attempt to abolish the Encomienda system. Spanish colonists largely ignore them. Spain backs down.
1550

Las Casas vs. Sepúlveda on the humanity and rights of Native Americans. No clear winner, but the debate shaped Spanish colonial law for decades.
1587
Roanoke Colony
England's first serious colonization attempt. Sir Walter Raleigh. Everyone disappears. Only clue: the word "CROATOAN" carved into a post.
1607
Jamestown Founded

DBQ Prompt
Evaluate the extent to which the Spanish colonization of the Americas in the late 15th and 16th centuries transformed the lives of indigenous peoples.

Document 1

Source: Bartolomé de las Casas A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies, 1542
"The Spaniards have shown not the slightest consideration for these people, treating them (and I speak from first-hand experience, having been there from the outset) not as brute animals — indeed, I would to God they had done so and had shown them the consideration they afford their animals — so much as piles of dung in the middle of the road."

Document 2

Source: Hernán Cortés Letter to King Charles I of Spain, 1520
"This great city of Tenochtitlan is built on the salt lake... The city has many squares where markets are held and trading is carried on. There is one square twice as big as that of Salamanca, with arcades all around, where more than sixty thousand people come each day to buy and sell..."

Document 3

Source: Laws of Burgos Issued by the Spanish Crown, 1512
"We order and command that henceforth no person or persons shall dare to beat any Indian with sticks, or whip him, or call him dog, or address him by any name other than his proper name alone; and if an Indian deserves to be punished for something he has done, the said person having him in charge shall bring him to the visitor for him to punish as he sees fit."

Document 4

Source: Indigenous Oral Account (recorded by Spanish missionaries) Nahuatl Lament, c. 1528 — Mexico
"Weep, weep for Tenochtitlan, where we once saw water and solid ground. The walls are black, our city is in ruin. Our shields gave it no protection. We have chewed on hard lumps of mud, lumps of mud and salty sedge and wormwood."

Document 5

Source: Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda Democrates Alter, c. 1544
"The Spanish have a perfect right to rule these barbarians of the New World and the adjacent islands, who in prudence, skill, virtues, and humanity are as inferior to the Spanish as children to adults, or women to men, for there exists between the two as great a difference as between savage and cruel races and the most merciful of peoples."
Scoring Breakdown (7 points possible)
  • Thesis (1 pt) — Makes a historically defensible claim that responds to the prompt. Must do more than restate the prompt.
  • Contextualization (1 pt) — Describes a broader context accurately related to the prompt. Must relate it to the argument. Pre-contact Native societies, European competition, or Columbian Exchange work here.
  • Evidence: Document Content (2 pts) — Uses content from at least 3 docs to address the topic (1 pt), or uses content from at least 6 docs to support your argument (2 pts).
  • Evidence: Outside Evidence (1 pt) — Uses relevant historical evidence not in the documents to support your argument. The Encomienda System replacing itself with the asiento system, or specific disease mortality rates, are strong outside evidence choices.
  • Analysis: Sourcing (1 pt) — For at least 3 documents, explains how or why the document's point of view, purpose, historical situation, or audience is relevant to the argument.
  • Complexity (1 pt) — Demonstrates a complex understanding. Comparing transformations across different regions, or explaining why indigenous response varied, earns this point.

LEQ Prompt
Evaluate the extent to which the Columbian Exchange transformed Native American societies between 1492 and 1607.

Scoring Breakdown (6 points possible)

  • Thesis (1 pt) — A historically defensible thesis that makes a claim and establishes a line of reasoning. "The Columbian Exchange transformed Native American societies significantly, primarily through devastating population collapse from disease, but also through the introduction of new animals and trade goods that some groups turned to their advantage."
  • Contextualization (1 pt) — Must describe the broader context and explain how it relates to the argument. European motivations (Three Gs), the economic theory of mercantilism, and the pre-contact state of Native societies all work here.
  • Evidence (2 pts) — 1 pt for specific relevant historical evidence. 2 pts for using that evidence to support the argument. Names, dates, specific outcomes matter here.
  • Analysis: Historical Reasoning (1 pt) — Uses comparison, causation, or continuity and change over time to frame the argument. Causation is the obvious move here: disease → population collapse → labor shortage → African slavery.
  • Complexity (1 pt) — Demonstrates sophisticated understanding. One good move: argue that the Exchange's effects were uneven — catastrophic for some groups, but transformative in other ways (horses on the Plains, metal tools in trade networks).

Evidence to Use

DiseaseSmallpox, measles, typhus. Some estimates say 50–90% population loss in Caribbean within decades of contact. Aztec empire weakened by epidemic before Cortés even arrived.
AnimalsHorses reach the Great Plains and transform Plains cultures that had previously been semi-settled. By the 1700s, nations like the Comanche are built around horse culture. The Columbian Exchange gave them the horse.
Labor SystemsPopulation collapse → Encomienda breaks down → Spanish shift to African slave trade. The Columbian Exchange's disease effects directly caused the asiento system.
Trade GoodsMetal tools and European goods enter Native trade networks and become status items. Some groups gained power by controlling access to European goods. Exchange isn't purely negative.

Your Essay

Shoot for ~4-5 paragraphs. Intro (thesis + context), 2-3 body paragraphs with specific evidence, no formal conclusion necessary — end on your strongest point.

SAQ Prompt — No Thesis Required
Use the following excerpt and your knowledge of United States history to answer parts A, B, and C.
Source: Bartolomé de las Casas A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies, 1542
"The reason the Christians have murdered on such a vast scale and killed anyone and everyone in their way is purely and simply greed. They have set out to line their pockets with gold and to amass private fortunes as quickly as possible so that they can then assume a status entirely at odds with that into which they were born. Their insatiable greed and overweening ambition know no bounds."
Part A
Briefly explain ONE specific historical development that supports Las Casas's argument about Spanish motivations for colonization.
Part B
Briefly explain ONE specific development that complicates or challenges Las Casas's characterization of Spanish colonization as purely driven by greed.
Part C
Briefly explain ONE way in which Native American peoples responded to Spanish colonization in the period 1492–1607.
SAQ Tips (3 points — 1 per part)
  • No thesis. No intro. Just answer the question directly and specifically.
  • Part A ideas: The Encomienda System's forced labor structure, the Spanish casta system designed to extract wealth from colonial subjects, conquistadors like Cortés explicitly seeking gold and tribute.
  • Part B ideas: Missionary activity (Franciscans, Jesuits), the Valladolid Debate showing genuine religious debate within Spain, the Laws of Burgos attempting (if failing) to protect native people, Spain's stated goal of Christianization as a legitimate motivation beyond greed.
  • Part C ideas: Pueblo peoples' resistance through syncretism (blending Catholic and indigenous religious practices), diplomatic negotiation and trade as adaptation strategies, armed resistance in smaller scale conflicts, or the alliances some groups made with Spanish against rival indigenous nations.
  • Each part needs a specific example with historical context. Vague answers don't score.
